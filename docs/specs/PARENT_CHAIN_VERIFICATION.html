Proving the Parent Chain

Consensus cannot see the chain a swap is paid on, so a miner can take the escrow without paying. Four ways out, and the one fact about your setup that rules two of them in and two of them out.

What is fixed, and what is not

SwapAccept closed front-running: entitlement to an open swap is now block data, so nobody can name their own address after someone else has paid. What it does not do — and its own commit message says so — is prove anyone paid on the parent chain at all. That is the subject of this memo.

02 — The pivotal fact

Your mainchain is not your parent chain

The cheap fix in L1_PAYMENT_PROOFS works because the node already holds a proof-of-work-validated header chain. It holds exactly one, and it is not the one you want to swap against.

Coinshift is merge-mined against eCash, so the BIP300 enforcer feeds it eCash headers, which land in the archive's main_header_infos with ancestry enforced on insert. Your parent chain is BTC. Those are two different chains, so BTC headers are not there and no amount of refactoring will put them there.

This is the whole difficulty, and it is easy to miss because eCash is a BTC hardfork — same header format, same SHA-256d work function. Sharing a format is not sharing a chain. A BTC header supplied to the node hashes to a block the archive has never heard of.

So "focus on BTC only" does not simplify the problem. It rules out the option that costs a fortnight and leaves the two that cost more.

Why the raw header is supplied at all

BlockHeaderInfo is {block_hash, prev_block_hash, height, work}. It carries no merkle root, so the archive alone cannot check inclusion. Having the claim supply the raw 80 bytes and checking that its hash is already known closes that gap without touching the enforcer — the proof carries its own trusted root. That part of the design is sound and stays sound in every option below.

03 — The failure

What a miner can do today

No lie to any RPC is required. Consensus simply never asks.

validate_swap_claim_consensus checks that the claim spends an output locked to its swap, that it pays the address a live reservation names, and nothing else. connect then explicitly trusts the block and advances local swap state to match. Honest nodes will not relay the claim — but relaying is not required in order to mine it.

block data — every node agrees node-local observation — nodes differ the theft path

04 — Option A

Swap against the mainchain

trustless no new data source 1–2 weeks gives up BTC

Included as the baseline the other two are measured against, and because it is the only option available today. If you accept XEC as the swap asset, L1_PAYMENT_PROOFS Option B works as written with nothing new to trust: the claim carries a proof, and consensus authenticates the header against the archive it already validated.

You have said the parent chain is BTC, so treat this as the fallback rather than the plan — but it is worth knowing that the fully trustless version of this product is a fortnight away in one direction and months away in the other.

The payment commits to the swap with an OP_RETURN carrying "CSFT" | swap_id | l2_claimer_address — 56 bytes, inside the 80-byte relay limit. This is the elegant part: consensus reads the claimant out of the commitment and pays that address, so entitlement follows from the act of paying, which is the only thing that should ever have established it.

The catch, stated plainly

This makes the swap an XEC ↔ Coinshift instrument. That overlaps the BIP300 two-way peg you already have — but not uselessly: it is the fast, peer-to-peer version of it, with no withdrawal-bundle wait. Whether that is the product is a question for you, not for the code.

05 — Option B

Relay BTC headers into consensus

trustless-ish real BTC months relay must be kept alive

Everything in Option A, plus a way to get an authenticated BTC header chain into L2 consensus state. Headers arrive as ordinary L2 transactions; consensus validates proof-of-work and the retarget schedule, and keeps the heaviest chain.

BTC is the best possible case for this. Forging a proof means outrunning BTC's hashrate, which is the strongest assumption available anywhere — unlike BCH or LTC, where renting a majority is cheap enough that fake proofs are a real business. And because eCash is a BTC fork, the header format and SHA-256d work check are shapes your node already handles; BTC's 2016-block retarget is simpler than eCash's ASERT.

A hazard your fork relationship creates

eCash descends from BTC, so the two chains share every block up to the 2017 fork and their headers are structurally identical. A relay anchored at genesis could therefore be fed eCash headers and accept them as BTC — and an attacker who can mine eCash cheaply would then be forging BTC inclusion proofs.

The relay must be checkpointed well past the fork height and validate BTC's retarget specifically, so that a chain following the other schedule cannot satisfy it. This is cheap to get right and expensive to discover late; it belongs in the design from day one.

06 — Option C

Never look at BTC at all

trustless real BTC smallest change taker must run swap software

The whole verification problem exists because the protocol asks "did a payment happen on another chain?" — a question consensus cannot answer. An atomic swap never asks it. The information travels through a secret instead of through an observation.

This is one substitution away from your own step 3. You said the maker must be the one to advance the release, and that instinct is right — it just wants to be a revealed preimage rather than a signature, because a preimage is self-proving and a signature needs someone to decide when it is owed.

What it costs in the codebase

There is no scripting engine and no timelock primitive today — OutputContent is Value | Withdrawal | SwapPending. You would add one variant, roughly HashLocked { value, hash, claimant, refund_to, timeout_height }, with two spend rules: claimant-with-preimage, or refund-after-height. No interpreter, no relay, no oracle. On the BTC side nothing is built into consensus at all — the wallet constructs and watches a standard P2WSH HTLC.

SwapAccept survives, doing what it is actually good for: matching a taker to a posted order on-chain, so you keep open orders rather than needing an out-of-band handshake.

The real trade

The taker can no longer pay from any wallet or an exchange withdrawal. They must run software that builds an HTLC. That is the single question this option lives or dies on.

07 — Option D

The attestation ladder, and why it does not close

not trustless adds latency worth understanding

Your sketch — register the payment after a BMM, advance depth with another, have the maker release with a third, add three more attestations if he withholds — deserves a proper answer rather than a dismissal, because the shape of why it fails is the most useful thing in this memo.

One correction first: step 1 does need a block. The maker's escrow is not locked until SwapCreate is mined; there is no extra wait, but there is a block.

The real problem is at 2.1. "Coinshift checks the RPC and registers the block and confirmations" — which Coinshift? Every node asks its own endpoint. Writing that answer into consensus is precisely the bug that was fixed this week, promoted to a design principle. The only escape is to put the registration in a transaction — at which point it is an assertion by whoever mined it, and a miner attesting to their own claim is Figure 2 with extra steps.

The trap inside "the maker must release"

Requiring the maker's signature makes the swap a 2-of-2 escrow, and then the timeout branch has no safe assignment. Refund to the maker and he takes the BTC and stalls. Release to the taker and she never pays and waits. Every fixed rule favours one side, because the chain cannot tell a withheld release from a payment that never came.

09 — Regardless of which

Most of the work is the same either way

A useful property of A and B: they differ in exactly one module. You can start now without settling the decision.

Shared by both, and worth doing on its own merits:

  • the L1PaymentProof type, its Borsh encoding, and merkle verification with the CVE-2012-2459 duplicate-hash guard
  • the deterministic mainchain-height watermark, so confirmation depth means the same thing on every node
  • getting a header view down to the transaction validators — the spec calls this the awkward piece, and it is: the view lives in Archive, validation sees only State
  • the OP_RETURN commitment format and parsing
  • moving swaps_by_l1_txid uniqueness into consensus state, written by connect and reversed by disconnect
  • removing update_swap_l1_txid, and with it the fabricated-fill footgun the integration tests currently rely on

What differs is only where an authenticated header comes from — the archive, or a relayed chain. Put that behind one interface and the choice stays open until the rest is built.

Option C shares less: it needs the HashLocked output and its two spend rules instead. But it also deletes more, including the entire observer and every trust question attached to it.

10 — The decision

One question settles it

How does the taker pay?

Everything above collapses to this. It is a product question, not an engineering one, which is why it is yours rather than mine.

They run our software

Take Option C. Trustless, no relay, no oracle, no new trust assumption, and it removes the parent-chain observation problem instead of mitigating it. Keep SwapAccept for matching and the RPC observer as pure UI. This is my recommendation.

Any wallet or exchange

Then BTC facts must reach consensus, and that is Option B — months, with a relay to keep alive and the fork-checkpoint hazard to get right. No cheaper path exists that is honestly trustless.

With the parent chain confirmed as BTC, Option A is off the table unless the asset itself is negotiable, and Option D does not close the hole. So the live choice is B or C, and the two differ far less in security than in what you ask of the person on the other side of the trade.

In the meantime

Whichever way this goes, the README says "trustless" about a leg that currently is not. Until one of these lands, either say so plainly or ship a bonded attester set and describe it as the federation it would be. Being wrong in the documentation is the one option with no upside.
